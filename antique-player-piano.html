What Kind of Player Piano Do You Have?

Most people who call us have inherited an instrument and do not yet know what it is. There is one question that splits the whole field: is there a paper roll behind a panel above the keys? If yes, you have a pneumatic player — it runs on suction, not electricity, and it is almost certainly worth saving.

Pneumatic

Standard Player Piano

A paper roll, a vacuum system, and (on many) two foot pedals you pump. The player plays whatever the roll says at whatever volume you pedal. The most common thing we are called out to.

Reproducing

Ampico

American Piano Company's reproducing system. Encodes the original pianist's dynamics, so it recreates a performance rather than just the notes. Found in Chickering, Knabe, Mason & Hamlin and others.

Reproducing

Duo-Art

Aeolian's reproducing system, and the main rival to Ampico. Fitted to Steinway, Weber and Steck instruments among others. Worth identifying before anyone opens it up — these are the valuable ones.

Reproducing

Welte-Mignon

The German original, and the system that started reproducing pianos. Rarer in the States and mechanically distinct from Ampico and Duo-Art, so it needs someone who knows the difference.

Piano Makers

Aeolian, Gulbransen & Nordiska

These names are on the fallboard, not the mechanism. Gulbransen built the famous "easy to pedal" players; Aeolian built both pianos and the Duo-Art system. The nameplate and the mechanism are often two different companies.

Coin-Op

Nickelodeons & Orchestrions

Same pneumatic principles with added ranks, percussion, and a coin mechanism. More moving parts, same leak-hunting approach.

Have a modern electronic system instead — a screen, an app, or a box under the keybed? That is a different animal: see Disklavier, PianoDisc, QRS or Spirio.

Common Issues

Why Antique Players Stop Playing

Nine times out of ten the piano is fine and the pneumatic system has aged out. All of the below are repairable.

Perished Tubing and Leaking Bellows

The single most common failure. Original rubber tubing and bellows cloth harden, crack, and leak after several decades. A pneumatic system that cannot hold suction cannot play — and every other symptom usually traces back here.

Dead Notes While Others Play

Individual pouches or valves have failed rather than the whole system. The player works, but a handful of notes never sound. Tedious to trace, straightforward to fix.

Roll Will Not Track or Advance

Tracking mechanisms drift out of alignment and take-up spools slip. The roll wanders, tears at the edges, or stops feeding entirely. Usually adjustment rather than replacement.

Pedals Pump but Nothing Happens

You are moving air and it is escaping before it reaches the notes. Points to a major leak in the exhausters, the reservoir, or the main supply — and it is the symptom people most often assume is fatal. It usually is not.

Electric Vacuum Motor Dead or Screaming

On instruments fitted with a suction box, motors seize, bearings dry out, and switches fail. Repairable or replaceable without touching the pneumatics.

It Plays, but the Piano Sounds Awful

The player is doing its job and the piano underneath needs tuning, regulation, or hammer work. Easy to mistake for a player fault — and much cheaper to fix.

FAQ

Antique Player Piano Questions

How do I tell what kind of player piano I have?
Look for a paper roll behind a hinged panel above the keys. If there is one, it is a pneumatic player driven by suction, not electricity. A nameplate on the fallboard or inside the lid usually names the piano maker (Aeolian, Gulbransen, Nordiska), while the player mechanism itself may be a different brand entirely — Ampico, Duo-Art, or Welte-Mignon. Send us a photo of the nameplate and the roll mechanism and we will tell you what you have.
What is the difference between a player piano and a reproducing piano?
A standard player piano plays the notes on the roll at whatever volume the pedaller chooses. A reproducing piano — Ampico, Duo-Art, Welte-Mignon — also encodes the original pianist's dynamics and pedalling, so it recreates an actual performance with nobody at the bench. Reproducing pianos are more complex, more valuable, and worth identifying correctly before anyone works on them.
My player piano does not play. Is it worth fixing?
Usually the pneumatic system has failed rather than the piano. Original rubber tubing, pouches, and bellows cloth harden and leak after several decades, and a system that leaks cannot hold the suction it needs. That is repairable. We assess the instrument and tell you what has failed and what it costs before you commit to anything.
Do you work on coin-operated nickelodeon pianos?
Yes. Nickelodeons and orchestrions run on the same pneumatic principles as a home player piano, with added ranks, percussion, or coin mechanisms. They need the same leak-hunting approach, just with more moving parts.
Can you still get player piano rolls?
Yes — original player piano rolls turn up constantly at estate sales and online, and new rolls are still being cut. If your instrument will not play the rolls you already own, that is a tracking or suction problem on the piano's side, not a supply problem.
How much does antique player piano repair cost?
Every job is quoted in writing before any work starts. Smaller repairs are quoted per job. A full restoration typically runs $8,000 to $15,000 in labor, depending on the instrument and its condition.
